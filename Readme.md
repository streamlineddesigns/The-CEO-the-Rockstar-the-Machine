       The CEO, the Rockstar, the Machine
       A Story of Code, Chaos, and the Architecture of Memory
       
          Prologue: The Ending
       The severance agreement sat on the screen like a tombstone with bullet points.
       Twenty-four items. Twenty-four pieces of intellectual property, each one representing months or years of obsessive work, sleepless nights, sticky notes covered in equations, and moments of genuine breakthrough — all reduced to a numbered list on a legal document with a DocuSign envelope ID.
       The contractor stared at it for a long time. Not because he didn't understand what it meant. He understood perfectly. It meant that everything he had built, everything he had dreamed about building, and everything he had sacrificed to keep building, was now officially his. Not because anyone had recognized its value. But because the people who owned the company had so thoroughly failed to understand what they possessed that they were willing to trade it away for the price of a used sedan and some back wages.
       Five years. Over five years of his life, poured into a company that never once put his name on a single document that mattered. And now, in the final act, they had handed him the keys to the kingdom while keeping the castle — not realizing the castle was empty without what powered it.
       He signed the agreement.
       Then he closed his laptop, sat in the silence of his home, and thought about what to do next. Not with the IP. Not with the technology. But with the story. Because the story, he realized, was the thing that no one had ever heard. And it was the thing that made everything else make sense.
       
          Part One: The Medic
       
              Chapter 1: Sand and Sirens
       The man who would become the CEO of Color Surge was raised by his mother. His father was present for a while, and then he was present in the way that certain fathers are present — technically nearby, practically corrosive. Soon enough, the family decided they were better off as three: the mother and her two boys.
       The CEO — though he was not yet a CEO, not yet anything resembling one — grew up in Southern California with the kind of quiet desperation that doesn't announce itself. It just sits in the room with you. He did not know what he wanted to do with his life. This is a common affliction among young men who grow up without a model for what a life well-lived looks like. The absence of a blueprint does not make you free. It makes you paralyzed.
       So he joined the Army.
       He became a combat medic. He deployed to Iraq. He learned, in the most visceral and irreversible way possible, what it means to keep someone alive when everything around you is engineered to do the opposite. There was a day — and I heard this story only once, secondhand, told casually over coffee as though it were a minor anecdote — when an IED detonated beneath his unit's vehicle. He survived. He helped others survive. The details beyond that are his to keep.
       What I can tell you is what he brought home: a stillness. A kind of earned calm that comes from having stood inside the worst thing you can imagine and walked out the other side. That stillness would serve him well. It would also, eventually, make him difficult to read. When things got bad at Color Surge — and they got very, very bad — it was hard to tell whether he was strategizing or simply dissociating. Maybe both. Maybe that's what leadership looks like when you've been through what he'd been through.
       After Iraq, he returned to Southern California and discovered that the civilian economy did not particularly care about his ability to stabilize a sucking chest wound under fire. He cleaned pools. He parked cars. He survived, which is what he was trained to do, but surviving is not the same as living.
       Then one evening, his roommate showed him something on a laptop screen.
       His roommate was building video games.
       
              Chapter 2: Tinker Toys
       The roommate's games were small, scrappy things — the kind of projects that live and die on a single weekend of inspiration. But the CEO watched him work, and something clicked. Not the mechanics of code — he was not a programmer and never would be, not really — but the architecture of play. The way a simple rule set could produce emergent complexity. The way constraints bred creativity. He recognized this. It was the same principle that governed tactical medicine, that governed survival itself: you don't need every tool. You need the right tool, applied with precision.
       He found a platform called BuildBox, a no-code game development engine that let designers build functional prototypes without writing a single line of code. He read two books that would reshape his thinking: Tinker Toys and The Art of Game Design. The first taught him to look at unrelated objects and find structural commonalities. The second taught him to look at game mechanics and find emotional resonances.
       He started building.
       His first game was a flop. It took too long, cost too much, and required the help of an outside engineer who charged premium rates for mediocre work. The CEO absorbed this failure the way he absorbed everything — quietly, completely — and then he simply did not repeat the mistake. From that point forward, it was just him and his roommate. Two men and a laptop.
       He began producing games at a pace that bordered on compulsive. One per month, sometimes faster. Most were forgettable. But he was learning. Each iteration refined his instinct for what he called "the hook" — that invisible thing that makes a player tap one more time, survive one more level, refuse to put the phone down even though they've been sitting on the toilet for twenty minutes and their legs have gone numb.
       Then, one afternoon, he combined two ideas that had no business being together.
       He took the simple mechanic of a ball bouncing through obstacles — a mechanic so primitive it could have been sketched on a napkin — and he layered on top of it a color-matching system that required split-second pattern recognition. The obstacles rotated. The ball changed color. You had to pass through the segment that matched your current color or you died instantly. There was no health bar. No second chances. Just a binary: match or die.
       He played the prototype for hours without stopping.
       He knew.
       Every designer has that moment — the moment when the thing you're building stops being an experiment and starts being the thing. He was holding it. A jewel-toned, seizure-inducing little gem that hummed with potential energy. He called it Color Surge.
       
              Chapter 3: The Publisher
       The CEO took Color Surge to every publisher he could reach. They all passed. The feedback was some variation of "too simple" or "the market is saturated" or "we don't see the revenue model." Standard publisher cowardice. The kind of institutional risk-aversion that has killed more great products than bad design ever has.
       Then he met Z.
       Z was a man of considerable ambition and flexible ethics. He appeared through a connection — connections being the currency of the mobile gaming world — and he liked what he saw. More importantly, he saw what the publishers had missed: Color Surge was not a game. It was a compulsion loop disguised as a game. It was pure dopamine engineering. It was beautiful.
       Z made a proposal. He was building a publishing company — Fortress Games — and assembling a small team. He had a social media marketer named Sam with genuine talent for viral content. He had an advertising specialist named Mark, who understood user acquisition funnels the way the CEO understood game design. Together with the CEO and his roommate, they formed the founding team. Five people. Two business entities. One game.
       This arrangement — a game developer contracting with a separate publishing entity — was not unusual. In fact, it was the standard model. The same way the largest homebuilders in America don't actually build houses; they coordinate networks of independent contractors who each own their own business. It is efficient. It is scalable. It is also, depending on the character of the people involved, either a brilliant partnership or an elaborate mechanism for exploitation.
       Whether Fortress's arrangement with the CEO was one or the other would become, over the next several years, a question worth tens of millions of dollars. But in the beginning, it was simply five people trying to make a small ball bounce through spinning circles without anyone's phone exploding.
       The game launched. The team added levels — "Challenges," they called them — to complement the original endless mode. They iterated. They marketed. They grew.
       And then Kanye West took a screenshot.
       
              Chapter 4: The Accident
       The story of how Color Surge went from a moderately successful indie game to a global phenomenon is, like most origin stories of enormous cultural moments, profoundly stupid.
       Kanye West was building his own app at the time. He took a screenshot of his phone to show it to his audience. In the screenshot, Color Surge was sitting at the number one spot on the App Store. His app was at number two. He posted the screenshot without specifying which app was his. Millions of people — possessed by the deranged collective intelligence of social media — assumed it was Color Surge.
       In less than twenty-four hours, the game received over one million downloads.
       The App Store's algorithm, which rewards momentum with visibility, took over from there. Color Surge rocketed into the stratosphere. It was the kind of growth that venture capitalists dream about and engineers have nightmares about, because suddenly the infrastructure that was designed for thousands of users had to serve millions, and no one had prepared for that.
       The team scrambled. More levels were added. The game evolved from a single endless mode into a sprawling collection of challenges, each level a bite size snack of the classic mode. BuildBox, the no-code platform that had birthed the game, began to creak under the weight of the ambition piled on top of it. The team needed real engineers now. Not hobbyists. Not consultants. Real, proper software engineers who could take the game from its prototyping environment into something scalable and sustainable.
       Mark, the advertising specialist, had a solution. He was founding his own development studio — EyeCube Games — based in Vietnam. An overseas team. Cheap, capable, and available immediately.
       The CEO watched this unfold with mixed emotions. On one hand, he was getting the engineering support he needed. On the other, he was watching another member of his team build a business inside his business, using his product as the foundation. The distributed team model that Z had proposed was revealing itself, piece by piece, not as a partnership of equals but as a series of nested dependencies, each layer extracting value from the one beneath it.
       But the game was making money. Serious money. And money has a way of making structural concerns feel academic.
       For two years, this arrangement held. Color Surge grew to over thirty game modes. The revenue was significant. The team was productive. The publishers renegotiated their contracts — always upward, always justified by costs that the CEO couldn't independently verify — and the CEO's share shrank in proportion.
       Then the contract came up for renewal.
       The CEO did something that required the same kind of quiet, calculated courage that had gotten him through Iraq. He said no.
       He refused to renew with Fortress Games. The option was in the contract. It was his right. He exercised it.
       Fortress's response was immediate and brutal. They pulled Color Surge from the App Store.
       The CEO sued. Fortress countersued — from Dubai, where the legal landscape was more favorable to their position. What followed was a court battle that would stretch across seven years, span multiple jurisdictions, and cost enough in legal fees to fund a small country's education budget.
       Color Surge would eventually win. But "eventually" is a word that covers a lot of suffering.
       
          Part Two: The CTO
       
              Chapter 5: The Gaming Store
       During the years of litigation, Color Surge needed to stay alive. Revenue had to continue flowing. The product had to evolve. And the CEO and his business relations officer — a man named Chris who handled the operational and legal machinery of the company — needed a technical leader.
       The CEO found one in an unlikely place.
       He walked into a gaming store one afternoon — the kind of place that was half retail shop, half mini-arcade, with glowing screens and the faint smell of energy drinks and overheated GPUs. The owner was a man we will call Joe.
       Joe was charismatic in the way that certain people are charismatic: not through warmth, exactly, but through a kind of focused intensity that made you feel like you were the only person in the room. He knew games. He knew the culture. He knew how to talk about technology in a way that sounded authoritative without being technical. The CEO dropped ten thousand dollars at Joe's store that day — a line of credit to come back and buy whatever he wanted — and in doing so, began a relationship that would shape the next six years of Color Surge's existence.
       Joe became the CTO.
       This is the part of the story where, if you were reading it as fiction, you might expect me to describe how Joe's technical brilliance transformed the company. How he built systems and wrote code and pushed the product to new heights.
       That is not what happened.
       What Joe did — and I need to be careful here, because the full picture only emerged years later, assembled from fragments and contradictions and late-night confessions — was something more subtle and, in its own way, more impressive. He built a management layer. Not of processes or procedures, but of information asymmetry.
       He stood between the owners and the engineers. He stood between the CEO and the code. And he made himself indispensable not by producing work, but by controlling the flow of credit for work that others produced.
       But I'm getting ahead of myself. Because to understand what Joe controlled, you first have to understand who was actually doing the work.
       
              Chapter 6: The Contractor
       The contractor arrived in the summer of 2020.
       I learned about him the way I learned about most things in this story — in pieces, over time, from sources who each held only a fragment of the whole picture. What follows is my best reconstruction.
       He was young. Mid-twenties. No degree. No pedigree. No network. What he had was a GED, a juvenile record that included the FBI showing up at his grandparents' house, and approximately five years of professional experience that read like a picaresque novel: Linux administrator, front-end developer, lead game developer at a startup that imploded, and then a solo freelancer building everything from websites to virtual reality games out of a bedroom.
       He also had something else, something harder to quantify. A capacity for obsession that bordered on the clinical. When he found a problem interesting, he didn't work on it. He inhabited it. He would spend his free time daydreaming about code. He would lie awake at night reverse-engineering systems he'd read about in blog posts and conference talks. He would piece together how competing companies built their proprietary technology by listening to engineers brag at conferences and extracting tiny details from their boasts — details that, to a knowledgeable ear, were essentially blueprints.
       He arrived at Color Surge through Joe. A year earlier, Joe had hired him for a small contracting gig: clone the game Color Surge. The contractor did it, got paid, and moved on. Then, in June 2020, Joe called him back. He said he was now an executive at Color Surge. He said the contractor could skip the interview process. He said he was pre-vetted.
       The contractor had other options. There was an interview at Apple. There was a VR project he owned outright — a game where you could fly like a bird, which had caught the attention of Meta's Oculus programs. But Joe was persuasive. Joe was always persuasive. And the contractor was hungry. Not for money, exactly — though he needed that too — but for belonging. For a place where his work would matter. For a home.
       He took the job.
       
              Chapter 7: The Arrangement
       The first thing the contractor learned was that Color Surge was in a fight for its life.
       The lawsuit with Fortress was ongoing. Legal costs were bleeding the company. Revenue needed to not just continue but grow, and it needed to grow with a skeleton crew because the company couldn't afford to hire a full team.
       The second thing he learned was the organizational structure. Or rather, the absence of one.
       The CEO and Chris were largely absent — handling legal matters, managing the business from a distance, trusting Joe to run the technical side. Joe was nominally the CTO. Under Joe were the engineers: the contractor and a couple other developers. Everyone else had already been let go.
       The third thing the contractor learned — though this took considerably longer — was that Joe was not what he appeared to be.
       On the surface, Joe was the technical leader. He assigned projects. He set priorities. He reported progress to the owners. He was the interface between the people who wrote code and the people who owned the company.
       Beneath the surface, Joe was running a different operation entirely. He took the contractor's ideas and presented them as his own. He took the contractor's technical proposals — detailed, researched, sometimes brilliant — and relayed them to the owners as though they had sprung fully formed from his own mind. He kept the contractor isolated from the owners, maintaining a wall of separation that served his interests perfectly: the owners thought Joe was doing the technical work; the contractor thought Joe was the only path to recognition.
       The contractor didn't figure this out for years. In his defense, he had other things to worry about.
       He was drowning in debt. He had children. He was being paid a contractor's rate while doing the work of a CTO, a lead engineer, and a research scientist combined. Joe's salary was roughly ten times his own. And Joe — this is a detail that the contractor assembled only in retrospect, piecing it together the way he pieced together everything — was taking a portion of the Color Surge budget allocated for the contractor's compensation and spending it in Las Vegas. The contractor received whatever was left over.
       The contractor stayed anyway. He stayed because Joe kept promising that the lawsuit would end, that the money would come, that Color Surge was going to be the last job he ever needed. He stayed because he believed, with the fervor of someone who had spent his entire life being told that the good times were just around the corner, that if he just worked harder, just built one more system, just solved one more impossible problem, everything would finally be okay.
       And so he worked. He worked on the projects Joe assigned. He worked on the projects he suggested to Joe — projects that Joe would inevitably present as his own ideas before assigning them back to the contractor. And he worked, in every stolen hour and sleepless night, on projects that no one had asked for. Projects born from his obsession. Projects that would, years later, turn out to be among the most innovative work produced in the mobile gaming industry.
       He just didn't know it yet.
       
          Part Three: The Machines
       
              Chapter 8: Boids
       It started with birds.
       Not real birds. Simulated ones. Boids — a flocking algorithm created by Craig Reynolds in the 1980s, back when the idea of making digital creatures behave like living things was the cutting edge of computer science.
       The concept is deceptively simple. You give each bird in a flock three rules: don't crowd your neighbors, steer toward the average heading of your neighbors, and move toward the average position of your neighbors. From these three rules, complex, organic-looking flocking behavior emerges. No central controller. No master plan. Just local interactions producing global order.
       The contractor was fascinated. But he was also, even at this early stage, thinking about performance. The naive implementation of boids is computationally brutal. Every bird has to compare itself to every other bird, every frame. Ten birds? Fine. A hundred? Sluggish. A thousand? Your phone catches fire. In computer science terms, this is O(n²) — the kind of scaling behavior that makes engineers sweat.
       He had been reading about spatial partitioning — a family of techniques for dividing space into regions so that objects only need to compare themselves to nearby neighbors, not everything in the universe. He understood the theory. Spatial hashes, quadtrees, octrees — he could explain them fluently. But he couldn't build one. He had never seen working code, and he lacked the confidence to translate his theoretical understanding into implementation.
       This is a common paradox among self-taught programmers. You can hold the entire architecture of a system in your head, rotate it, examine it from every angle, and still feel that the gap between understanding and implementation is uncrossable. It's a confidence problem, not a competence problem. But it feels indistinguishable from incompetence when you're sitting alone at a desk at 2 AM.
       One day, he found a partially completed spatial hash written in Java on an obscure forum. The implementation was simple enough that he could follow it, though it used mathematical operations he didn't immediately understand. So he did what he always did when confronted with something he couldn't intuit: he got out a pencil and a piece of paper and worked through it by hand.
       He drew a grid. He labeled cells. He plotted random points and traced, step by step, how a 2D coordinate could be converted into a cell ID. Column from the x-coordinate. Row from the y-coordinate. Scale by cell size. The formula was straightforward — different from the one on the forum, but functionally equivalent.
       He plugged his spatial hash into the boids simulation. He modified the nearest-neighbors search to use hash lookups instead of exhaustive comparisons. He stopped updating every frame, instead updating every n frames.
       Suddenly, a thousand boids were trivially possible.
       Something clicked for him in that moment — a connection that would reverberate through everything he built afterward. He remembered hearing someone state a golden rule of programming: any O(n²) operation can be reduced to O(n) by using a hash map instead of nested iterations. At the time, it had sounded like a platitude. Now, watching his boids swarm fluidly across the screen at a thousand strong, it became visceral truth. The spatial hash's O(1) key-value lookups meant that finding nearby neighbors was effectively instantaneous. The bottleneck wasn't computation anymore. It was architecture.
       He refined the spatial hash further. His original key-ID formula worked only in positive integer space. He rewrote it to use vector keys instead — dividing each coordinate by cell size and truncating to integers. The point (50, −30) with a cell size of 10 maps to cell (5, −3). The point (55.5, −33.3) also maps to (5, −3). Clean, elegant, and extensible to any number of dimensions.
       He didn't realize it at the time — he wouldn't realize it for years — but he had just built the fundamental primitive that would underlie every major system he would create for Color Surge. Spatial partitioning. Decompose the problem space, then conquer each piece separately.
       The same principle that would later lead him, accidentally and inevitably, to reinvent one of the core architectural innovations behind the most powerful AI systems on Earth.
       
              Chapter 9: The Lag Problem
       The contractor's first significant contribution to Color Surge was solving a performance crisis that had plagued the game for years.
       Color Surge ran on Unity, the game engine. On high-end devices, it was smooth. On mid-range devices, it was acceptable. On low-end Android phones — the kind used by a significant percentage of Color Surge's global player base — it was, at certain moments, unplayable.
       The contractor had been profiling the game's performance, looking for the source of the lag. He'd made several incremental improvements — the kind of optimizations that many competent developers had missed, the high-hanging fruit that accumulates in any codebase. But the lag persisted. Not constantly, but in spikes. At specific, predictable moments.
       One late night, he saw it. Deep in the profiling data, buried under layers of other metrics, was a spike in CPU usage tied to collision detection. It occurred whenever the player passed through groups of circles.
       He dug deeper.
       Each circle in Color Surge used polygon colliders to match its visual shape precisely. Every circle had four colored segments — four polygon colliders, each defined by twenty vertices. For a simple level with a few circles, this was fine. For a complex obstacle — circles nested inside circles, stacked in a row — the numbers became grotesque. Three nested circles times four colliders equals twelve polygon colliders. Four such stacks means forty-eight colliders. At twenty vertices each, that's 960 vertices participating in real-time collision detection. Every frame.
       The contractor's solution was, of course, spatial partitioning.
       He inserted all obstacles into a spatial hash. He identified the nearest obstacle to the player. Then he inserted that obstacle's collider coordinates into a second spatial hash. A k-nearest-neighbors query returned the relevant colliders, which he enabled; all others remained disabled.
       The performance gain was not incremental. It was categorical. Low-end devices went from unplayable to smooth. On a mid-range PC, the contractor's stress-test scene had previously capped at thirty complex obstacles before becoming a slideshow. After the spatial hash integration, hundreds of obstacles ran without any performance degradation.
       This was the contractor's first taste of something that would define his entire tenure at Color Surge: the experience of solving a problem so thoroughly that the problem ceased to exist. Not a workaround. Not a mitigation. An elimination. The kind of engineering that makes the game appear, to the end user, as though the problem had never existed in the first place.
       He would chase that feeling for the next five years.
       
              Chapter 10: Procedural Content Generation
       The second major project was procedural level generation.
       Color Surge's levels — "Challenges" — were manually designed. A human level designer determined which obstacles appeared in each level, what order they appeared in, and what difficulty they represented. This was fine when the game had twenty levels. It was burdensome when it had a hundred. It was untenable when the team wanted thousands.
       The contractor was tasked with automating this process. The number of times he returned to the drawing board was, by his own accounting, staggering. The final solution, however, seems almost elegant in retrospect.
       Obstacle Encoding. Each obstacle in Color Surge had a unique configuration: colors, colliders, behaviors, names. To use obstacles in an algorithm, the contractor needed to encode them numerically. He assigned each a numeric ID, then cast that ID to a character. Each obstacle now had a single-character representation that was both human-readable and computationally useful.
       Level Encoding. Levels became concatenations of their obstacle characters — strings representing each level's composition.
       Similarity Scoring. The character encoding enabled something crucial: similarity scoring between levels. Generated levels needed to be unique, not duplicates of existing ones. The contractor implemented three string-similarity algorithms — number of common characters, longest common substring, and longest common subsequence — and combined them in a weighted average. Each level could now be compared against every other level, producing a similarity percentage.
       Difficulty Scoring. This was where the project stalled.
       Levels could be generated by randomly selecting obstacles until a low-similarity candidate emerged. But this produced random difficulty. Levels were supposed to be grouped by difficulty. The contractor needed a way to assign difficulty scores to individual obstacles, then sum them per level to get level difficulty.
       But how do you assign difficulty to an obstacle when difficulty is subjective, contextual, and has never been explicitly labeled?
       The contractor shelved the problem. He didn't have the tools.
       Then he learned about neural networks.
       
              Chapter 11: Neurons and Evolution
       A friend once told the contractor that neural networks are "just graphs." Did he understand graph theory? He knew the basics. Nodes, edges, traversal. With that mental model, he dove into the literature.
       He started with the most fundamental unit: the single-layer perceptron. The underlying math was y = mx + b. The exact equation that every high school student dismisses as useless turned out to be the atom from which all of deep learning was built.
       Training via gradient descent was beyond him at this stage, so he turned to genetic algorithms — an optimization technique inspired by biological evolution. The concept: randomly initialize a population of weight arrays. Evaluate each set of weights on a task. Keep the best performers. Discard the rest. Cross-splice the survivors to produce offspring. Repeat. Over hundreds or thousands of generations, the weights converge toward a solution that minimizes error.
       It is a slow, brute-force approach. It is also extraordinarily intuitive. You don't need to understand calculus to understand natural selection.
       The contractor implemented a genetic algorithm to train perceptrons. It worked. He turned to the XOR problem — the classic demonstration of what single-layer perceptrons cannot learn due to nonlinearity — and built a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) to solve it. He fit that MLP to XOR twenty times, not because he needed to, but because the experience of watching a machine learn a nonlinear function from nothing was, to him, a kind of magic.
       Then he brought the neural network back to the procedural content generation problem.
       Feature Vectors. He viewed each obstacle's configuration as a data point. Colors, colliders, names, behaviors — these became dimensions of a feature vector. He normalized these vectors using min-max scaling, mapping every value to the range [0, 1].
       Difficulty Inference. He framed the problem as multivariate linear regression: multiply a weight vector against each obstacle's normalized feature vector, add a bias term, and the output is a difficulty score. The weight vector was the learnable part.
       Reinforcement Learning. The contractor didn't have explicit difficulty labels (supervised learning), nor a clear reward signal (reinforcement learning). What he had was the existing ordering of levels by difficulty — a human-generated ground truth about which levels were easier and which were harder.
       If obstacle difficulty scores summed to level difficulty scores, then a genetic algorithm could optimize the weights by scoring how closely the algorithm's predicted level ordering matched the true ordering. The fitness function was ordering accuracy.
       He prepared the feature and weight vectors, plugged in random values, and observed: 15% accuracy. Randomly guessing the correct order of a hundred-plus levels was, unsurprisingly, terrible. He hand-tuned weights: 35%. Then he ran the genetic algorithm.
       He watched accuracy climb. 40%. 55%. 68%. It plateaued around 70%.
       For the easing function governing the difficulty curve — how difficulty should increase across levels within a game mode — he discovered that mathematical easing functions (the same curves used for animation timing) closely approximated the real difficulty distribution when parameterized with the min and max difficulty scores. An easing function sufficed.
       The generation pipeline came together: encode obstacles, compute feature vectors, run inference to assign difficulty scores, order levels, extract min/max scores, compute target difficulty using easing functions, select candidate obstacles, place them until target difficulty is reached, compare against existing levels for similarity, keep or regenerate. Loop until entire modes were populated.
       But 70% accuracy wasn't enough. The contractor had two more ideas.
       
              Chapter 12: The Accidental Hypernetwork
       The first idea was straightforward. Rather than replace the perceptron with the MLP, what if the MLP generated the weights for the perceptron?
       He implemented this. The MLP consumed the same inputs — feature vectors, centroids, context — and output a set of weights. Those weights were then used by the perceptron to score obstacles. The perceptron was now a function of the MLP.
       Accuracy climbed to around 85%, sometimes 87% with patience.
       It would be years before the contractor learned that what he had built had a name. In the academic literature, a neural network that generates the weights of another neural network is called a hypernetwork. He had reinvented it from first principles, in a mobile gaming sandbox, while trying to make procedurally generated levels feel fair.
       He did not know the term. He did not know the literature. He just needed a network to produce another network, so he built one.
       The second idea was semi-supervised learning, though he knew what that was called.
       He hypothesized that if he hand-labeled a subset of obstacles as easy, medium, or hard, and added a classification loss term to the fitness function alongside the ordering accuracy, the signal would improve. He was injecting partial supervision into what had been a pure reinforcement learning problem.
       Accuracy rose to 92%. More importantly, the generated levels consistently matched their target difficulty. That small injection of labeled data — what a researcher would call "semi-supervised learning" — was the difference between a system that mostly worked and one that reliably worked.
       He considered the PCG project complete.
       
              Chapter 13: K-Means and the Beautiful Accident
       Before finalizing the procedural generation system, the contractor had taken a detour that seemed unproductive at the time but would prove foundational later.
       He had implemented K-means clustering — an algorithm that groups data points into K clusters based on feature similarity. He'd read about its ability to partition min-max scaled feature vectors, which was, in fact, one of the reasons he'd created them in the first place. One day, without reference code, he implemented K-means clustering, including K-means++ initialization, on his first attempt.
       He initially set K to 3, expecting the algorithm to split obstacles into three clean groups: easy, medium, and hard. It didn't. The clusters bore no obvious relationship to difficulty.
       Then he increased K.
       What happened next is something the contractor still talks about with a quiet reverence.
       With higher K values, the clusters stopped corresponding to difficulty and started corresponding to visual similarity. Obstacles that looked alike — that shared structural features, color patterns, rotation behaviors — clustered together with striking precision. He stared at the output. The algorithm had, without any visual input whatsoever, using only the numerical feature vectors he'd constructed, organized the obstacles into groups that a human level designer would have instinctively recognized as "the same kind of thing."
       He stared at it for a long time.
       Then he set it aside. It solved nothing for difficulty scoring.
       But it planted a seed. The idea that clustering in feature space could reveal structure invisible to the designer — that the data contained relationships no one had explicitly encoded — would return. And when it returned, it would change everything.
       
              Chapter 14: The Bot
       The contractor had proven that machine learning could generate levels for Color Surge. Now he wanted to prove that machine learning could play them.
       The motivation was partly scientific and partly strategic. Rovio Studios — the makers of Angry Birds — had publicly discussed combining procedural content generation with ML-powered bots to automatically playtest generated levels. If Color Surge could demonstrate the same capability, it would represent a significant technical achievement. The kind of achievement that impresses investors. And Color Surge, still bleeding from the Fortress lawsuit, needed to impress investors.
       The contractor assumed this would be quick. If genetic algorithms could train neural networks to play Flappy Bird — a game with one input and one mechanic — they could surely handle Color Surge.
       He was wrong.
       Color Surge was a fundamentally harder problem than Flappy Bird. The obstacles rotated at varying speeds. The color segments required precise timing. Some obstacles had randomly changing parameters. And — most critically — many obstacles that looked identical required different actions depending on context that was only discernible through temporal information: velocity, recent actions, upcoming patterns.
       He tried reinforcement learning with feedforward neural networks. The bots evolved. They learned to survive easy obstacles. Watching a bot pass a level for the first time was — he described it as startling. The same involuntary flinch you get when something impossible suddenly happens in front of you. But performance plateaued. The bots could not surpass a certain score.
       He tried behavioral cloning — recording his own gameplay as demonstrations and training a network to imitate his actions. This required careful engineering of the observation space. At any moment, the optimal action depended not just on what the player could see, but on the player's velocity. Jumping might be correct at low velocity and catastrophic at high velocity. He didn't initially include velocity data. It took him a year to discover, through Unity ML-Agents documentation, that spatio-temporal features like velocity were critical for this class of problem.
       With velocity included, the behavioral cloning bot outperformed his reinforcement learning bot. A pure feedforward network scored around 40 before dying. Remarkable for an early attempt, but nowhere near human performance.
       He switched to Unity ML-Agents, which provided access to LSTMs — recurrent networks with memory — trained via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). He spent the next five months in what he later described simply as "intense."
       He used approximately 128 observations with 32 timesteps for the LSTM. He trained multiple bots. Each one learned to pass certain obstacles but failed on others. The next training run would learn different subsets. No single network achieved general competence.
       This was the fundamental problem. Each LSTM was a monolithic function approximator trying to learn the entire state space simultaneously. When the space contained obstacles with fundamentally different dynamics — some requiring fast reflexes, some requiring pattern recognition, some requiring pure timing — a single network couldn't represent all strategies at once without sacrificing performance on each.
       The contractor needed a different architecture. He needed a way to decompose the problem space and assign specialized networks to each region.
       He needed, though he didn't know it yet, a Sparse Mixture of Experts.
       
              Chapter 15: The Ensemble of Ensembles
       The solution arrived through a convergence of ideas that the contractor had been accumulating for years.
       He knew that ensembling — averaging predictions from multiple models — consistently improves accuracy when individual models exceed 50% accuracy. This is well-established in the literature.
       He trained multiple LSTMs independently, each producing slightly different competencies. His plan was to average their outputs using learned weights — a technique known as non-naive bagging.
       But here is where the contractor's obsession with spatial partitioning led him somewhere unexpected.
       Remember the K-means clusters? The ones that had beautifully organized obstacles by feature similarity? The contractor decided to use those clusters as a routing mechanism. Instead of building one ensemble that averaged over all obstacles, he built one ensemble per cluster. An ensemble of ensembles.
       Each ensemble was referenced by its cluster ID. At inference time, the current obstacle was classified by its cluster, and the corresponding ensemble handled the prediction. The clusters partitioned the obstacle space. The ensembles specialized within each partition.
       The contractor called this an "ensemble of ensembles." He viewed it as spatial partitioning in feature space: obstacles with nearby feature vectors were processed by the same ensemble; distant obstacles went to different ensembles.
       He did not call it a Sparse Mixture of Experts because he did not know what a Sparse Mixture of Experts was.
       The routing mechanism was a 1-nearest-neighbor classifier. Given an obstacle's feature vector, find the nearest centroid, route to that centroid's ensemble. The search used precomputed embeddings cached in a lookup table — the same spatial hashing principle he'd been applying since the boids simulation.
       The results were immediate and striking. Individual LSTMs learned limited subsets. The "ensemble of ensembles" did not have this limitation. It could play through high numbers of levels and achieve solid scores on endless mode. More importantly — and this is the result that still gives the contractor goosebumps — when he deployed the system in a game mode similar to Classic mode, it performed as though it had been trained on Classic mode. No previous architecture had achieved this.
       The system could play five different game modes.
       The contractor had achieved emergent generalization.
       He didn't understand why. He just knew it worked. And it had a practical advantage: if a cluster gained new obstacles, only one ensemble needed retraining. Underperforming networks within an ensemble could be replaced independently. Ensembles trained in parallel, one per server.
       He would not understand the theoretical significance of what he had built until much later. Until a leaked document described the architecture of GPT-4.
       
              Chapter 16: The Leak
       The GPT-4 architecture leak circulated through the machine learning community in early 2023. The contractor, who by this point had developed a habit of reading everything he could find about large language models, obtained a copy.
       He read through it methodically, the way he read everything — slowly, with a pencil nearby, translating each concept into terms he could relate to his own work. When he reached the section describing the model's use of Sparse Mixture of Experts in the context of multi-head attention, he stopped.
       He read it again.
       He read it a third time.
       Then he sat very still for a long time.
       The architecture described in the leak — a system where inputs are routed to specialized sub-networks based on binding affinity, where each sub-network processes only the inputs most relevant to its learned specialization, where the outputs are combined through a weighted aggregation — was, at a high level, structurally identical to his "ensemble of ensembles."
       His system used LSTMs, not self-attention. His routing used Euclidean distance to precomputed centroids, not learned gating networks. The implementation details were entirely different. But the principle was the same. Decompose the problem space via clustering. Route inputs to specialized experts. Aggregate outputs through learned weighting.
       He had independently reinvented the Sparse Mixture of Experts.
       He had done it in a mobile gaming sandbox, using tools he built himself, motivated by the practical necessity of making a small ball jump through spinning circles. And the same principle was now powering one of the most sophisticated artificial intelligence systems ever created.
       The contractor sat with this realization the way he sat with most realizations: quietly, with a mix of vindication and vertigo. The vindication came from knowing that his instincts had been correct all along — that spatial partitioning, applied recursively and at every level of abstraction, was not just an optimization technique but a fundamental principle of intelligent computation. The vertigo came from the scale of the gap between what he had built and where the idea had ended up. From a Unity project on a laptop to a model trained on billions of dollars of compute.
       He allowed himself a single observation, which he would later write down:
       "Sometimes the best discoveries aren't planned. They're just the natural consequence of refusing to accept 'impossible' as an answer."
       Then he went back to work.
       
          Part Four: Memory
       
              Chapter 17: Q Networks and the Black-and-White Trick
       The contractor could not stop thinking about the Color Surge problem. Not the business problem — though that would eventually consume him too — but the technical problem. The machine learning problem. Why was it that certain algorithms could achieve mastery in so many games but not this one?
       He went back to first principles.
       The Atari paper — "Playing Atari with Deep Reinforcement Learning" — had made headlines about AI teaching itself to play from scratch, beating human experts. But when the contractor actually read the paper carefully, instead of skimming the headlines, he noticed something the coverage had omitted: super-human performance was not the standard being achieved. Some games resisted the algorithm entirely. Others plateaued well below human performance. The RL community's poster child for general game-playing AI was, in reality, a highly specialized system that worked brilliantly on some tasks and failed quietly on others.
       This made the contractor feel slightly better about his own results. The Sparse MoE bot could outperform a measurable percentage of human players. That wasn't nothing.
       But it wasn't enough.
       He decided to go simpler before going bigger. Q-Networks — the tabular precursors to Deep Q-Networks — represented the most fundamental form of value-based reinforcement learning. A dictionary mapping observations to action values. No neural network required.
       The challenge was the observation space. Q-Networks require discrete, searchable states. Raw pixels are continuous and high-dimensional — exactly the wrong kind of input.
       The contractor's solution was characteristically pragmatic. He created what he called the "black-and-white trick." He placed a grid around the player and performed collision detection at each grid cell. A hit registered as 0; no hit registered as 1. This produced a binary, low-resolution representation of the game state — a manually convolved image, essentially — that was both quantized and searchable.
       This representation served two purposes. First, it could be used as input to a Q-Network, since the binary grid could be hashed into discrete keys. Second, it could be used as input to a CNN, since it was structurally identical to a black-and-white image.
       He combined this with a spatial hash. As the game was played, binary observations were inserted into the hash. When a similar observation recurred, the stored action values were retrieved. An epsilon-greedy strategy balanced exploitation of known-good actions with exploration of new ones.
       The result was a functional Q-Network that learned from pixel-like data without being prohibitively slow. The dimensionality reduction of the binary encoding was doing most of the heavy lifting — collapsing the continuous visual space into a manageable discrete one.
       Then the contractor tried something that, by his own description, gave him goosebumps.
       He wrote code to temporarily disable the bot at the start of each game. While the bot was disabled, the contractor played in its place. His gameplay — his observations and actions — were inserted into the Q-Network's memory. When the bot was re-enabled, it wasn't starting from scratch. It was starting from his experience.
       The bot learned to play in real time.
       Not the painstaking trial-and-error of standard reinforcement learning. Not the slow grind of gradient descent. The bot was, in real time, retrieving the contractor's stored experiences and using them to make better decisions immediately. It was behavioral cloning via associative memory, and the speed of adaptation was unlike anything he had seen in any of his previous experiments.
       He was startled. And, for a reason he couldn't articulate, slightly frightened.
       He deleted the code and moved on.
       
              Chapter 18: The Attention in the Room
       The contractor's next leap came from an unexpected direction: transformers.
       He had been studying the "Attention Is All You Need" paper — the 2017 breakthrough that had reshaped the entire field of machine learning. He understood the architecture at a mechanical level: queries, keys, values, dot-product attention, multi-head projections. But understanding the mechanics and understanding the principle are different things.
       The principle clicked when he looked at his own Q-Network setup and realized that something resembling attention was already present.
       His spatial hash had a search function. In database terminology, the function was called a query. The input observation was the query passed to the spatial hash. What the query was compared against were the keys. What got returned were the values.
       Query. Key. Value.
       The contractor stared at his own code with the unsettling recognition of someone who has been speaking prose his entire life.
       His setup was functionally performing attention — or something structurally analogous to attention — over a memory bank of past experiences. But it lacked the critical component that made transformer attention powerful: learned projections. His keys were manually defined. His queries were raw observations. There was no learned transformation that could push dissimilar concepts apart or pull related concepts together.
       This is where the architecture diverged from standard attention and became something new.
       In a transformer, queries, keys, and values are all linear projections of the same input. They live in the same representational space, and the projections are learned jointly through backpropagation.
       The contractor's system was different. He had a frozen visual encoder — a CNN autoencoder that mapped raw pixels to a latent representation. This encoder produced the keys. The keys were static. Once an observation was encoded and stored, its key never changed.
       Then he added a second, much smaller network — a query encoder — that also processed raw observations but produced a different projection. This projection was added residually to the frozen latent. The result was a query that lived in the same space as the keys but was shifted by a learned offset.
       The attention computation was cosine similarity between queries and keys, normalized into weights, applied to retrieved values.
       The critical insight — the one that made this architecture work where standard approaches failed — was the decoupling. The visual encoder produced stable keys. The query encoder learned to shift queries relative to those keys. Keys never moved. Only queries did.
       This meant that stored memories remained valid indefinitely. In Neural Episodic Control (NEC) — the most relevant prior work — the encoder was trained jointly with the rest of the system, meaning that as the encoder changed, the keys for previously stored memories became stale. The memories invalidated themselves through the very process of learning. The contractor's architecture avoided this entirely by freezing the encoder.
       He called the whole thing Associative Recall Memory. ARM. The building block of what would become a simulation of cognition. ARM was never referenced officially as this, but internally, it was sometimes simply called “The Machine”.
       The architecture was performing attention over episodic memories, retrieving past experiences based on learned similarity, and combining them with current observations to produce actions. It was, in computational neuroscience terms, a rough approximation of how biological memory is theorized to work: a visual system that processes current input, an attention mechanism that queries stored experiences based on learned relevance, and a retrieval process that surfaces associated actions.
       The contractor found it elegant. He also found it frightening, though he couldn't explain exactly why. There is a difference between building something that works and building something that thinks, and the contractor was not always certain which side of that line his creation occupied. 
       
              Chapter 19: HyperShot and the Latent MoE
       The contractor did not get to fully implement ARM. He got distracted.
       The distraction was a paper called "HyperShot: Few-Shot Learning by Kernel HyperNetworks." He found it while researching hypernetworks — a topic he'd become interested in after realizing that his PCG difficulty-scoring system constituted one.
       HyperShot's architecture was, to the contractor, revelatory. It learned visual embeddings for inputs with labels. Those embeddings formed a Gram kernel matrix — a similarity matrix between all pairs of inputs — which was fed into a hypernetwork. The hypernetwork generated the weights of a target network. The target network consumed a query embedding and the kernel, and output class probabilities via their indices.
       The contractor realized, with a jolt that he described as "goosebumps while writing about it years later," that HyperShot was performing something structurally similar to attention. It selected outputs via indices. It weighted based on similarity. It routed through a learned function.
       He also realized that HyperShot's support set — the set of labeled examples the hypernetwork was conditioned on — could be replaced with retrieved memories. Instead of a pre-computed training dataset, the support set could be whatever the associative memory bank returned as most relevant.
       This was the moment when the architecture began to compound.
       The contractor implemented a version of HyperShot as the query encoder in his ARM system. The hypernetwork was conditioned on the 1-nearest-neighbor centroid vector — the same routing mechanism from his Sparse MoE. It produced a target network that consumed the query representation and output action predictions.
       He called this integration Hyper-ARM. It was, he later realized, a latent Sparse Mixture of Experts. The hypernetwork generated different policy networks depending on which centroid the input was routed to. Each centroid's policy specialized on its region of the latent space. The experts were not stored as separate weight matrices — they were generated on demand by the hypernetwork.
       Before even connecting the memory module, the contractor tested the HyperShot query encoder with a residual connection back to the frozen latent. The results were startling.
       It outperformed every single network he had ever produced.
       Every feedforward network. Every LSTM. Every ensemble. The entire Sparse Mixture of Experts. The HyperShot-based query encoder, operating as a standalone policy network with a residual connection, produced higher scores than all of them.
       He couldn't delete these results. He had already pushed the code to branches and informed the executives of the solution he was testing.
       The answer, he eventually realized, was that he had built a latent Sparse Mixture of Experts. The hypernetwork wasn't just generating a single policy — it was generating different policies for different regions of the input space, conditioned on the centroid routing. This achieved the same specialization as his original "ensemble of ensembles" but with far fewer parameters, because the experts were generated rather than stored.
       The architectural implication was profound. If a monolithic network failed because it couldn't represent all strategies simultaneously, and a Sparse MoE succeeded by assigning specialized experts to sub-problems, then a hypernetwork-generated MoE achieved the same result while living entirely in the latent space. No separate weight matrices. No routing network. Just one hypernetwork and a set of centroids.
       
              Chapter 20: World-ARM — The Final Design
       The contractor never built the fourth version. But he designed it.
       The design emerged from a simple question: if ARM could learn to retrieve the right actions for a given visual state, could it learn to retrieve the right future states?
       A world model, in the machine learning sense, is a system that predicts what will happen next in an environment. Given the current state and an action, what will the next state look like? The ability to predict futures enables planning — evaluating sequences of actions before committing to any of them.
       The standard approach to world models is generative: train a neural network to hallucinate future states from scratch. This works, but it's fragile. Generated states drift from reality over long horizons. Small errors compound. The hallucinated future diverges from the actual future, and plans built on hallucinations fail.
       The contractor's insight was that memories could serve as starting points for prediction rather than requiring the model to generate from nothing. If the memory bank contained past state transitions — what happened after similar observations in the past — then the world model didn't need to hallucinate. It could remember.
       World-ARM would update the inputs and targets of the associative recall memory system. The memory module would store temporal observations paired with subsequent states and actions. The query encoder's input would become a latent image (from a VAE, not a standard autoencoder) concatenated with actions. The output would be the predicted next state — but grounded in retrieved memories rather than generated from learned parameters alone.
       The contractor sketched the design on notebook paper. He never wrote the code. He noted that the architecture was incomplete without Monte Carlo Tree Search — the planning algorithm that enables systems like AlphaGo to evaluate millions of possible futures — but he chose not to add it. Not because he didn't understand its importance, but because it wasn't the step he was on.
       "Version 5 irons out the wrinkles," he wrote. "Then MCTS goes on top, maybe hierarchical blocks, and we see the system really purr along. It's just not the step we're on."
       He also acknowledged, with a rare moment of self-aware humor, that his naming conventions were getting out of control. "World-Hyper-ARM? Hyper-ARM-World? The first sounds like buzzword vomit. The second sounds like a dimension of gym bros."
       
          Part Five: The Unraveling
       
              Chapter 21: The Grand Reveal
       When Color Surge finally won the Fortress lawsuit, the contractor had been with the company for nearly five years. Five years of building systems. Five years of promises. Five years of Joe standing between him and the owners, taking credit and redistributing scraps.
       Joe had a plan for the moment the lawsuit concluded. He was going to present himself to the owners as the architect of the company's technical transformation. He was going to claim the revenue gains — all of which were attributable to systems the contractor had designed and built — as evidence of his own value. He was going to leverage this into the ownership stake he had been promised: 25% of Color Surge's revenue. One-third ownership.
       A month before the expected resolution, the contractor had met Joe in Las Vegas to present his comprehensive machine learning strategy. The presentation covered everything: procedural content generation, difficulty scoring, player behavior modeling, dynamic difficulty adjustment, revenue optimization through A/B testing powered by ML, engagement prediction, churn prediction, user segmentation. Dozens of systems, each with multiple stages from simple to advanced, each designed to be iteratively refined, each equipped with machine learning capabilities at its advanced stage.
       Joe was, in the contractor's words, "baffled." He took the contractor out afterward and told him he had "finally made it." That everything up to this point had been "just getting started." That the contractor was "A Rockstar."
       Then Joe told him about the 25%. About starting a company. About giving the contractor his $150,000/year salary. About hiring engineers. About finally publishing and advertising Color Flow.
       The contractor allowed himself, for the first time in years, to feel hope.
       It lasted approximately six weeks.
       
              Chapter 22: The Fall of Joe
       In December 2024, Joe was fired.
       The specifics of what precipitated Joe's termination were, like most things involving Joe, layered in misdirection. What is known: Joe was offered $250,000 to walk away. He refused. Instead, he threatened the owners. This was an error of judgment so catastrophic that it suggests Joe had fundamentally misread the people he was dealing with. The CEO who had survived an IED in Iraq. The business relations officer who had navigated a seven-year international lawsuit. These were not people who responded well to threats.
       They did not respond at all. They had their lawyers respond. Then they discovered evidence of Joe's alleged embezzlement. And that was the end.
       Joe called the contractor. He told him that everyone at the company had been fired, including the contractor himself. This was a lie — the last in a long sequence of lies, deployed as a final act of leverage. If the contractor believed he was unemployed, he would be pliable. He would be desperate. He might side with Joe.
       The contractor did not side with Joe. Instead, he went above Joe's head for the second time in five years. He emailed the owners directly.
       They replied almost immediately.
       They flew him to Los Angeles.
       
              Chapter 23: Los Angeles
       The contractor landed in LA expecting to be fired. He figured the owners wanted information — details about what Joe had actually been doing for six years — and then they would cut him loose.
       What happened instead was a three-hour conversation that, piece by piece, dismantled every assumption the contractor had held about his own position at the company.
       The owners had not known who was doing the technical work. Joe had kept them in the dark. They thought Joe was building the systems. They thought Joe was the engineer. They had no idea that a contractor in Michigan — paid a fraction of Joe's salary, never given full-time employment, working on a laptop in a bedroom — was responsible for the procedural content generation, the spatial hashing optimizations, the ML bots, the revenue optimization systems, and a fully developed sequel game.
       The contractor presented the same comprehensive strategy he had presented to Joe in Las Vegas. The owners' reaction was, one imagines, considerably different from Joe's. Joe had been baffled because he was hearing a subordinate describe capabilities that exceeded his own. The owners were baffled because they were hearing, for the first time, a description of what their company actually contained.
       They put the contractor in charge.
       
              Chapter 24: The Sprint
       What followed was six months of implementation. The contractor took a handful of the most promising revenue optimization systems from his presentation — systems he had designed, prototyped, and in many cases fully built during his years under Joe — and deployed them to production.
       They worked.
       Not tentatively. Not incrementally. They worked the way the spatial hash had worked for collision detection — categorically. The systems were designed to be tweaked, adjusted, re-parameterized, and run again. Machine learning enabled faster, more predictable, more systematic optimization. This was the entire point. This was what every top-tier mobile gaming company was racing to implement. And the contractor had built it from scratch, alone, in a Unity sandbox, by reverse-engineering competitors' technology from conference talk transcripts.
       He was, for the first time, doing the work he had always known he was capable of. He was running the technical operation. He was making decisions that directly impacted revenue. He was sitting at the table.
       And then he made three mistakes.
       The first mistake was getting comfortable. After five years of working under Joe's thumb, the contractor allowed himself to relax. He slowed down. He stopped sprinting and started strolling. He prioritized his own projects over the company's immediate needs.
       The second mistake was drinking. He had developed a habit of drinking every evening — a habit he justified to himself with the logic that if Joe had gotten to slack off for five years while collecting a six-figure salary, then surely the contractor had earned the right to unwind. It was not good logic, but it was the kind of logic that feels ironclad at 11 PM on a Tuesday when you're three drinks in and staring at a screen full of code you've been staring at for half a decade.
       The third mistake was the phone calls.
       
              Chapter 25: The Phone Calls
       The contractor got very drunk and called the owners of Color Surge.
       He yelled at them. He shouted. He gave them, in his words, "a piece of his mind" about his compensation, his treatment, his value to the company, and the accumulated grievances of five years of manipulation, underpayment, and broken promises.
       He had learned, through years of working with Joe that yelling was sometimes the only language that produced results. It was not a lesson. It was a scar that looked like a lesson.
       The phone call did not produce results.
       What it produced was a demotion.
       The owners told the contractor that the company had decided to strip him of his access to financial systems and reduce his role. He protested. He argued that the demotion made no sense given that he was the one updating the revenue systems and monitoring them daily. He was the only person at the company who had successfully increased revenue.
       They kept saying his name. "Pierce." Just his name, over and over, in the tone that people use when they are trying to de-escalate a situation that they have already decided the outcome of.
       The following day, they sent a severance letter.
       
              Chapter 26: The Negotiation
       The contractor refused to sign the initial termination letter. It became litigious immediately.
       Here is what happened next, and I will describe it precisely because the details matter.
       The contractor contacted the California Labor Board. He discovered that the owners of Color Surge had violated multiple labor laws over the course of his five-year engagement. The details of those violations are beyond the scope of this narrative, but the financial consequence was clear: the company owed him in excess of $120,000 in back pay, and they owed it to him on the same day they were attempting to terminate him.

        ◦ 100% ownership of all AI Tools developed by the contractor during his employment. All twenty-four of them.           Part Six: The Man Behind the Machines
       
              Chapter 27: Las Vegas, Ditches, and the FBI
       To understand why the contractor made the choices he made — why he stayed with Joe for five years, why he got drunk and made the phone call, why he took IP instead of cash, why he could not stop building systems even when no one was paying him to — you have to understand where he came from.
       He was born in Las Vegas. He didn't stay long. His father liked to move. The moving was sometimes because of work that existed in gray areas, sometimes because warrants made staying inadvisable. Violence was part of the household from the beginning. Not constant, but present — the way weather is present. Some days were sunny. Some days, a backhand, a belt, or hands around a small neck. Typically while intoxicated.
       By the time the contractor was eight years old, living in Orange County, Florida, CPS had intervened enough times that he and his siblings were forcibly removed from their father's home. He remembers the police surrounding the house with weapons drawn. His father fled to the grandparents' house and hid on the roof. When the police left, he jumped off and broke both his feet.
       The grandparents took the children in. For the next decade, the contractor lived in a liminal space between two families, each of which resented the other. His father drifted in and out of jails and prisons — parole violations, substance abuse, violent incidents. Each reappearance brought promises. Each disappearance brought silence. Christmas was always over-the-top when his father was around. After a while, the children stopped asking for presents and started asking for him.
       The family moved to Michigan. The grandparents bought a house with settlement money from a workplace injury — flesh-eating bacteria in the grandfather's foot, an amputation, a $125,000 lawsuit. They settled in Traverse City, a small town in the northern part of the state.
       The contractor thrived in certain ways. He was gifted academically. So were his siblings; his brother & sister. He picked up skills quickly. He was physically daring — backflips, vaults, freerunning, climbing buildings and jumping off them. He was also, from the very first day of school in Michigan, a target. He and his brother, deeply tanned from years in Florida, were beaten by a group of children on their first day and called racial slurs. This group would continue to bully him through elementary school, middle school, and high school.
       Around the age of thirteen — he can never quite pin down the exact age, because that period of his life is a blur of overlapping crises — his father moved into the grandparents' house. What followed was a pattern that is both sadly common and uniquely horrifying in its specific details.
       One day, an argument between his father and grandparents escalated beyond the usual yelling. His father grabbed a large knife from the kitchen counter. He held it to the grandfather's throat. Then to the grandmother's rib cage. Back and forth. The contractor watched, paralyzed, then grabbed the telephone and dialed 911. His father saw him. He threw the knife into the wall — he was, the contractor noted with the peculiar detachment of someone recounting trauma, extraordinarily talented at knife throwing — turned to his thirteen-year-old son, and said, "I will kill you."
       He charged. The contractor ran. He made it to a neighbor's house and called the police. They arrived. His father was arrested.
       What followed was a falsification of the police report by the grandparents, who exaggerated the injuries and the number of blows to ensure additional felony charges. The father, who might have received a jail sentence, received a prison sentence instead. The contractor didn't understand the discrepancy between what he'd witnessed and what was in the report until years later, when his father confronted his grandmother about it during a prison visit and she broke down crying.
       The contractor spent his adolescence oscillating between grief, rage, and a keyboard.
       He discovered computers. He discovered programming. He discovered offensive cybersecurity — the art of breaking into systems. The glow of a monitor at 2 AM. The sound of keystrokes in an empty room. The elegance of a well-crafted exploit. He went from dabbling to proficient to dangerous with a speed that alarmed the adults around him.
       He changed his entire sophomore class's grades to prevent anyone from failing. He did it again at his next school. Then the FBI showed up at his grandparents' house — not about the grades, but about an attempted intrusion into a government system. A dam control system in Ohio. The contractor had targeted the server for fun, without checking what it controlled. The FBI was investigating potential terrorism. After some identify verification, they quickly shifted stances. They said the contractor was likely just some script kiddie.
       They searched his room. They found hand-written exploit code, original programs written from scratch on notebook paper. The agents' initial dismissal gave way to a more uncomfortable recognition: this teenager knew what he was doing. They seized everything.
       A computer forensics investigator named Todd visited him in juvenile detention. Todd gave him a book about programming in Perl. He pleaded with the contractor to "join the light side." To use his talents for something constructive.
       The contractor listened, eventually. Not immediately — he coded another exploit by hand in pencil while in detention, encrypted it manually, and ran it successfully the day he got out. The code? A web shell written in PHP like the C99 style scripts used to pop a web based shell on compromised servers — but eventually. He recognized that the path he was on led somewhere he didn't want to go. Todd's plea, combined with the very real experience of juvenile detention, created a pivot point.
       He started programming for real. Web development. PHP. The MVC pattern. Linux administration. He built a portfolio so technically impressive that a business owner looked at his work and said, "You must be some sort of prodigy or something." He got the job. Then another. Then another. Inevitably leading to that day Joe called him.
       
              Chapter 28: The Father
       There is one more piece of the story that needs to be told, and it is the piece that makes everything else make sense.
       When the contractor was twenty, his father got out of prison for the last time. The contractor moved in with him. His father was different — calmer, less volatile, seemingly changed. He was starting a business. The contractor was going to be his web developer. They found a lakehouse to rent. They moved in together.
       For a brief window, it seemed like things might actually work out.
       Then the contractor discovered that his father was using substances again. His brother found out first and was threatened into silence. When the contractor found out, he sat at the dining room table for days, unable to leave a notebook and pencil. He wrote obsessively about what he was supposed to do with his life.
       One evening, an argument escalated. The contractor said something sharp. His father grabbed him by the neck and pulled him out of a chair.
       What happened next is something the contractor has replayed thousands of times. The cast iron pan on the counter. His hand reaching for it. His hand shaking. Pulling back. His father's hand reaching for it. His father pulling back. The choke. The defensive move the contractor had been practicing — self-defense training he'd undertaken specifically because of his father. The punches. His brother leaping from the loft. The two of them pinning their father to the floor, terrified to let him up, knowing his anger wouldn't be extinguished by submission.
       The contractor called the police. When they arrived, his father was covered in bruises — the result of a medication that made him bruise easily, like a banana. The bruises made the contractor's story look like aggression. He was arrested. His father's story was believed.
       In jail, a guard whose own son had "beat up his dad" made a point of harassing the contractor. The contractor couldn't tell the guard or the police that his father was under the influence of hard drugs. Doing so would have violated his father's parole. The contractor — sitting in a cell, accused of a violence he had committed only in self-defense — chose to protect his father from a parole violation.
       His father came to the jail. Put money on his commissary. Told the police he wouldn't press charges.
       When the contractor confronted his father afterward about what had happened, his father blamed him. Said he didn't attack him. The contractor blamed himself for the rest of his life.
       Five months later, his father overdosed.
       The contractor does not believe it was accidental. He read the toxicology report. His father was on beta blockers that slowed his heart rate. He then took another substance known for slowing heart rate. Then he injected two typical doses of heroin simultaneously. His father had extensive knowledge of substances and dosing. The sequence was too precise to be careless.
       The contractor got the call at work. He had to keep working afterward.
       He does not discuss this often. When he does, it is with the clinical precision of someone who has rehearsed the narrative enough times that the emotion has been sanded away from the words, though not from the body. He cannot think about it without feeling a direct connection between his choice to pull away from his father after the fight and his father's choice to end his life. Choices like these and more comprised the majority of his thoughts for nearly a decade to come.
       This is the engine beneath everything. The obsessive work. The inability to stop building. The pathological need to prove value. The willingness to endure years of manipulation for the promise of belonging.
       He is not building machine learning architectures. He is building proof that he deserves to exist.
       
          Part Seven: The Architecture of Self
       
              Chapter 29: What He Built
       Let me now describe, with the precision it deserves, what the contractor actually created.
       He built a cognitive framework — a system of interlocking components that, taken together, constitute a rough computational analog of how biological memory is theorized to function. Not a metaphor. Not an approximation. A working implementation.
       A Visual Encoder that processes raw sensory input into a stable latent representation — analogous to the ventral visual stream transforming retinal input into object representations.
       An Attention Mechanism that learns to query stored memories based on functional similarity rather than visual similarity — analogous to hippocampal pattern completion, where a partial cue triggers retrieval of a complete memory.
       A Memory Bank that stores experiences as key-value pairs, indexed by visual latent and associated with actions and outcomes — analogous to episodic memory, which stores specific experiences bound to their context.
       A Routing Mechanism based on binding affinity to learned centroids — analogous to categorical perception, where the brain routes processing to specialized neural circuits based on the type of input.
       A Hypernetwork that generates specialized policy networks on demand based on routing decisions — analogous to the brain's ability to reconfigure its processing pipeline depending on task demands.
       A Decoupled Architecture where perception (the visual encoder) is frozen and attention (the query encoder) is learned independently — analogous to the separation between sensory cortex (which stabilizes in early development) and prefrontal cortex (which continues to adapt throughout life).
       A World Model design that grounds predictions in retrieved memories rather than hallucinating from scratch — analogous to constructive memory, where the brain reconstructs future scenarios by recombining elements of past experiences.
       Each component was derived from first principles. Each was implemented in a mobile gaming sandbox. Each was motivated by a practical engineering constraint.
       The contractor had a GED, a laptop, and the inability to accept "impossible" as an answer.
       
              Chapter 30: Recognition
       The contractor's work did not go entirely unnoticed.
       On a machine learning forum, he posted a description of his Associative Recall Memory architecture. Among the people who saw it was a man named Michal Valko — a researcher whose contributions to representation learning and self-supervised learning had helped solve the stability problems that made modern world models possible. Valko was not a minor figure. His work on preventing representational collapse — the tendency of self-supervised systems to converge to trivial solutions — was foundational.
       Valko reached out. The conversation, conducted over messaging, was brief and characteristic of the gap between the contractor's world and the academic one:
       "Hi Pierce, thanks for the question!"
       "Let's meet in ICML in person instead."
       "That would be awesome but I'm more of an ML Engineer than a formal researcher... I'm not sure if it would make it into ICML."
       The contractor explained his background. He described ARM, Hyper-ARM, World-ARM. He described the engineering-driven derivation, the mobile gaming sandbox, the absence of formal training.
       Valko's response was three emojis — a robot, a grin, and a lightbulb — followed by four words:
       "Let's work together then if you like!"
       The contractor was stunned. He asked what Valko had in mind. Valko replied: "Continuing on your ideas you outlined above."
       This was, for the contractor, the closest thing to external validation he had ever received. A world-class researcher had looked at work produced by a GED-holding, self-taught engineer in a Unity sandbox and seen something worth pursuing.
       The contractor began drafting a research paper. It was rough, informal, and structured more like an engineering document than an academic submission. But it existed. And it described, for the first time in formal terms, an architecture that its creator had been building, in one form or another, since the day he drew a grid on a piece of paper and plotted boid positions by hand.
       
              Chapter 31: Color Flow
       After the severance, the contractor took stock of what he had.
       He had twenty-four machine learning systems that he owned outright. He had Color Flow — a fully developed sequel to Color Surge, built on procedurally generated maps inspired by Flow Free, featuring five gameplay styles, automated testing with player progression analysis, and the Sparse Mixture of Experts code from his years at Color Surge. He had a fitness app. He had the early stages of a research collaboration with one of the most respected scientists in the field.
       He also had a family, accumulated debt, and no income.
       He spent six months trying to get his job back. He contacted the owners repeatedly. He wrote emails that oscillated between professional restraint and raw emotional honesty. He acknowledged the phone call. He acknowledged his behavior. He asked for forgiveness. He pointed out, accurately, that none of his systems had been replaced and that the company was still using them under the license granted by the severance agreement.
       The owners did not respond.
       The contractor then did what the contractor always did. He pivoted. He looked at his assets. He looked at the competitive landscape. He saw an opportunity that was, depending on your perspective, either brilliantly strategic or coldly ruthless.
       Color Surge had won the Fortress lawsuit. But the lawsuit had prior existing legal precedent about derivative works, intellectual property, and the rights of creators to evolve a genre. That precedent — established through cases like Atari Inc. v. Amusement World Inc., PUBG Corp. v. Epic Games, and Stardock Systems v. Reiche — carved out clear protections for independent creative works that share a genre but express it differently.
       Color Flow was not Color Surge. It was a different expression of the same genre. It was a "clean" asset — one that sat outside the litigation. And it came bundled with twenty-four AI systems that represented, in aggregate, a comprehensive machine learning infrastructure for mobile gaming.
       The contractor went to Color Surge's competitors.
       This was the group that had lost the multi-year lawsuit. The group that had been forced to pay millions. The group that had watched Color Surge win the legal fight that should have destroyed it.
       The contractor offered them everything he had won in the severance. The AI tools. Color Flow. The fitness app. The technical knowledge. The reverse-engineered revenue optimization systems. The procedural content generation pipeline. The Sparse Mixture of Experts. The cognitive architecture.
       He offered to build them a subsidiary. To run it himself. To take everything he had built for Color Surge and rebuild it under their banner.
       Before proceeding, he sent one final message to the owners of Color Surge. It was framed as an olive branch, but it read like an ultimatum:
       "If you want to prevent these assets from moving to a competitor and keep that market share consolidated, I'd need to see a concrete proposal before their review concludes at the end of this week."
       "Once I move forward with the other party and we begin actively migrating the fan base to the Color Flow ecosystem, the leverage shifts. If we reach the point where Color Flow has successfully captured that market, I will no longer be looking for an acquisition. At that stage, the only viable conversation will be a merger."
       The owners did not respond.
       
          Epilogue: The Mystery
       
              Chapter 32: Joe
       There is one final thread that must be addressed, and it is the thread that transforms this story from a narrative into something closer to a legend.
       Joe.
       Throughout this entire account, Joe has occupied a specific role: the manipulator, the credit-thief, the obstacle. He was the man who stood between the contractor and the owners for six years, siphoning value and misdirecting attention. He was, in the traditional structure of this kind of story, the villain.
       But the contractor doesn't see it that way. Not entirely.
       During the drunken phone call with the CEO — the one the contractor regrets more than any phone call in his entire life — the CEO let something slip. Joe, he said, had been arrested by the FBI.
       This detail landed like a stone dropped into still water.
       The contractor thought back. The nights in Las Vegas. The strange gadgets Joe kept with him — night vision goggles, a lock-picking kit, a high-powered laser, a pistol. The way Joe always seemed to know more than he should about operational security. The way Joe's assignments always seemed to require, upon deep reflection, state-of-the-art solutions. Not moderately good solutions. Not workable solutions. The best solutions that current technology could produce.
       How could that be possible? A man who ran a gaming store, whose technical knowledge appeared to consist primarily of enthusiasm, consistently directed the contractor toward problems that demanded cutting-edge machine learning innovations. Everything from similarity scoring. To the spatial hashing. The procedural generation. The neural networks. The reinforcement learning. The mixture of experts. Each assignment, in retrospect, pushed the contractor exactly to the frontier of what was possible.
       The contractor did not resolve this mystery. He could not verify the FBI detail. He could not reconcile Joe's apparent technical ignorance with his uncanny ability to identify the right problems. He could not explain why a man whose visible skill set consisted of charisma and manipulation had steered him, over the course of five years, toward building a cognitive architecture that independently paralleled the most advanced AI systems in the world.
       He tried. He looked for patterns. He examined every interaction, every assignment, every offhand remark. The more he looked, the more the pattern seemed to emerge — and the less he could be certain he wasn't imagining it.
       In the end, the contractor chose to leave the mystery unresolved. To note it, acknowledge it, and set it down.
       Because the mystery of Joe is, ultimately, less important than what it produced.
       
              Chapter 33: The Architecture of Memory
       I began this story by saying that endings are never where you think they are.
       This story does not end with the contractor selling his IP to competitors. It does not end with the severance agreement or the phone call or the demotion. It does not end with the Sparse Mixture of Experts or the Associative Recall Memory or the World Model.
       It ends — to the extent that it ends at all — with a man sitting alone, staring at handwritten notes on sticky notes and scraps of paper. Solutions to complicated problems, worked out by hand, in pencil, the way he has always worked. He keeps these notes not because they are useful, but because they are proof. Proof that he was there. Proof that the work existed. Proof that someone, somewhere, with no degree and no pedigree and no institutional support, looked at the hardest problems he could find and refused to stop until he had solved them.
       Some of those solutions represent his peak engineering work. He knows this. He looks at them the way a retired athlete looks at game film — with pride, and grief, and the knowledge that those particular capabilities existed at a specific time and may never exist again in exactly that form.
       The systems he built were designed to be tweaked. To be adjusted and re-parameterized and run again. Machine learning was equipped to find solutions faster, more predictably, more systematically. This approach comes with a price: it requires abandoning the old mindset of "throw more pasta at the wall and see what sticks." Instead: if it doesn't stick, change the parameters and try again. You don't throw it away.
       Every top-tier company in the world races to build these kinds of systems. They are the only known mechanism for scaling intelligence. No one throws them away.
       But someone threw his away. Neatly compartmentalized and tossed in the trash. Every system he built. Every solution he dreamed up. Every late night and daydream and reverse-engineered conference talk. Discarded by people who did not understand what they had.
       The contractor carries this with him. Not as bitterness, exactly — or not only as bitterness. As something more complicated. A mixture of grief and pride and bewilderment that defies clean categorization.
       He once described the experience of building the Sparse Mixture of Experts — the moment when he realized that his "ensemble of ensembles" matched the architecture behind GPT-4 — as feeling like he had "accidentally 'made it' and then threw it all away." He said this with a laugh that was not quite a laugh. More like the sound a person makes when the absurdity of their own life becomes, briefly, funny enough to survive.
       And he said something else. Something that captures, better than any technical description, the essence of what drives him:
       "I worked like I was trying to keep my own company from being stolen. I worked like I was trying to make sure everyone at the company could keep their jobs. And I worked like my kids and my family's future was on the line."
       This is true. All of it. He worked exactly that way. Not because he was being paid to. Not because anyone asked him to. But because somewhere in the wiring of a mind shaped by juvenile detention centers and FBI visits and a father who threw knives and a grandmother who falsified police reports and a grandfather who drank himself to death after his wife died — somewhere in all that wreckage — a circuit formed that equated building with surviving.
       The architecture of his memory is not so different from the architecture he built. A visual encoder that processes the world. An attention mechanism that learns which memories matter. A retrieval system that surfaces associated actions. A routing mechanism that assigns specialized responses to different types of input. A decoupled system where perception is frozen — the past is what it is — and only the query changes. You can't change what you see. You can only change what you look for.
       He is still looking.
       He is still building.
       And the systems he built — the ones that were tossed in the trash, the ones that live on sticky notes and scraps of paper, the ones that exist only as rough drafts and proof-of-concept demos — those systems contain, encoded in their architecture, a principle that the largest AI companies in the world are spending billions of dollars to implement at scale:
       Decompose the problem space. Conquer each piece separately. Remember what worked. Forget what didn't. And never, under any circumstances, accept "impossible" as an answer.
       The contractor learned this principle in Traverse City, Michigan.
       He has been applying it ever since.
       
       Whether this story ends in triumph or tragedy depends, I think, on who is reading it. The contractor would prefer triumph. He has earned that preference. But he is honest enough — painfully, relentlessly honest — to acknowledge that the jury is still out.
       He once wrote, in a note to himself at the bottom of a technical document, a single line that serves as well as any as an epitaph for this particular chapter of his life:
       "I still have some of my notes saved, sticky notes, pieces of paper where I solved some of these complicated problems. Just so I can look at them and remember."
       He is remembering.
       And he is not done.
